Try the boulders

Every result on this page rests on four movement rules. Play them here: this board is the puzzle from the real-game video, and the engine is the Lean model nhMove transcribed to JavaScript.

Dlvl:1 $:0 HP:16(16) Pw:2(2) AC:10 Xp:1/0 T:1

Click the map, then use hjkl / yubn or the arrow keys. White text on the top line is the game's own message (from src/hack.c); the grey line under the map explains which rule fired.

Explained

The hero is a human Valkyrie whose only item is a (weight 500). The level carries the flag, kept on by a sealed (the ^ walled in on the right of the puzzle). Under those conditions:

  1. An orthogonal step into a boulder pushes it one square, if the square behind is floor without a boulder.
  2. A diagonal push is refused: The boulder won't roll diagonally on this floor.
  3. A diagonal step is refused when both corner squares are wall or boulder: You cannot pass that way.
  4. A blocked push does nothing. A light hero could squeeze onto the boulder's square; the loadstone makes that impossible.

Together these say the hero is the robot of . A diagonal walk through an open corner is just two ordinary steps. In the puzzle, try pushing the lower boulder east first: it lands on g against a wall and the indicator under the map flips to "no".

Full proof

The JavaScript move function follows nhMove in proofs/lean/NetHackPush.lean:146-183 branch for branch, in the same order as test_move: (1) target not free → refused, no time passes (hack.c:1003-1073); (2) diagonal with both corners bad rock → "You cannot pass that way." (hack.c:1153-1160; cant_squeeze_thru returns 3 under Sokoban); (3) boulder on target: if the square beyond is free and boulder-free, a diagonal key gives the "won't roll diagonally" refusal (hack.c:441-448) and an orthogonal key pushes (hack.c:631); otherwise "You try to move the boulder, but in vain." and cannot_push returns −1 for a heavy hero (hack.c:304-311); (4) else step. The "With great effort you move the boulder." message follows the real repeat rule (hack.c:180-197): shown for a new boulder or after a two-turn gap.

The reachability line runs a breadth-first search over orthogonal Push-1F moves from the current position. By reach_iff (Lean) that is the same set of positions the 8-direction NetHack keys reach.

Dlvl 1

The question

Given a level written in NetHack's own Lua level language on a map of any size, can the hero end a turn alive on target square g? The literal game is trivial because it ends after 109 turns, so the map size and turn cap are lifted.

Explained

Complexity theory classifies problems by how cost grows with input size. The real NetHack has a fixed 80×21 map, fixed-width integers and a hard stop: once the turn counter reaches one billion, the game ends with "The dungeon capitulates". A fixed-size game has a fixed (if astronomical) number of states, so any question about it is answered in constant time. That says nothing.

So the project studies a family: the same C code, with only the map width and height lifted, every other integer kept at its C width, and the turn cap removed. An instance is a straight-line Lua script built from a short of calls (map, boulders, loadstones, fortune cookies, a few traps), run on a fixed hero: a human female Valkyrie with no pet.

Randomness needs a rule too. NetHack rolls dice everywhere. Four readings were considered:

  • : the random seed is part of the input (models tool-assisted play; NetHack never saves its RNG).
  • : the player picks every die roll. "Can a lucky run win?" This defines the languages.
  • : the dice are an adversary. "Can the player force a win?"
  • : win with probability at least θ.

The hardness reductions are built so that dice cannot help or hurt (), which makes them hold under all four readings at once.

Dlvl 3

Hunger off: PSPACE-complete

A heavy hero under Sokoban rules moves boulders exactly like the Push-1F robot moves blocks, so reachability inherits Push-1F's PSPACE-hardness; and a game state never needs more than polynomial memory, so it is also in PSPACE. STRONG

Explained

Hardness. is a puzzle: a robot on a grid pushes one block at a time; some blocks are fixed forever; can the robot reach the target? Ani et al. proved it -complete in 2022. The draws any Push-1F board as a NetHack level: fixed blocks become walls, movable blocks become boulders, the robot becomes the hero. If the hero can do exactly what the robot can do, no more and no less, the two questions have the same answer.

"No more" is where NetHack fights back. A normal hero can squeeze onto a boulder's square when carrying almost nothing, and can slip diagonally between two boulders. The first attempt failed on exactly this (see Dlvl 7). The fixes: a that cannot be dropped keeps the hero too heavy to squeeze, and the flag forbids diagonal pushes and corner squeezes. That flag switches itself off once no pits or holes remain, so one sits sealed in the rock where nothing can fill it.

Then every other command has to be checked: kicking, praying, throwing, eating, sitting, travel, the m prefix. The proof walks through all of them. Prayer is the delicate one: an angry god can attach an iron ball and chain, and a lucky prayer can teleport a hero who is stuck in a wall.

Membership. With no monsters, no wishes and fixed-size counters, a game state fits in polynomial space. A machine can guess moves and dice one turn at a time, keeping only the current state; removes the guessing at a squared cost in space.

Machine check. The movement core, "one NetHack step equals zero, one or two Push-1F moves, and every Push-1F move is one NetHack step", is proved in .

Lemma E (enclosure).one step openreview-3-pspace.md:54-66

The Push-1F paper never says its board is finite or walled. Let R be the bounding box of the paper's drawing and B = R grown by one empty layer; outside B is fixed.

(a) P_B solvable ⇒ P solvable: every legal move in P_B is legal in P. The NO direction transfers unconditionally.
(b) If P has a solution in which no block ever leaves R, then P_B is solvable: the empty margin is a 4-connected ring, so any excursion outside B can be replaced by a walk along it.
(c) For Ani et al.'s reduction, (b) needs "every block stays inside its gadget's footprint in the intended solution". True by design, but still to be confirmed once against Figs. 32-37. If a figure lets a block escape, use a margin of k+2 cells.

Dlvl 4

Faithful hunger: NP-complete

Food is finite and prayer refills slower and slower, so (with rule S) every play lasts polynomially many turns: a winning play is a short certificate, which puts the problem in NP; boulder gadgets that encode planar 3-SAT make it NP-hard. STRONG

Explained

The clock. The hero starts with 900 nutrition and loses 1 per turn. A fortune cookie gives 40. A successful prayer while weak from hunger refills to 900, but each success raises the prayer timeout by (turns − 100,000)/100, capped at 32,767. After about 3.4 million turns a new prayer is possible only every 32,567 turns, while a refill buys at most 1,250 nutrition. Prayer can no longer keep up, so total play length is bounded by a polynomial in the number of cookies.

The loophole. Starvation is only checked when the faint roll fails. Under R2 the player chooses the roll, so the hero can faint forever and never starve. That makes hunger useless as a clock. closes it: the play is lost once nutrition drops below −(100 + 10·Con), the point where every real tick would kill with high probability. Without rule S the problem is PSPACE-complete again (Dlvl 2).

The puzzle. The source problem is : variables on a line, all-positive clauses above, all-negative below, drawn without crossings. The hero walks two phases. First, a P walk through each variable picks one branch, and every branch pushes boulders that close the clause paths of the literals it makes false. Then a K walk must pass every clause through some path that is still open. Where a K path must cross a P path, a crossover uses a hole: the K traveller plugs it with a boulder, once.

Completeness. Given a satisfying α: at each fork take the branch of α's value, closing only paths of false literals; walk the main line to x_E; in each clause take the first true leg (its W is still on N); at crossovers push δ, τ, plug; at doors push Kd. Every cell is entered at most twice. Eat a cookie on entering when hunger ≤ 860: hunger stays in [857, 900], never Hungry, rule S never fires. No step depends on the RNG.

Soundness. Gates (crossovers, doors, forward exits) split K into segments in one chain K₀ ≺ H₁ ≺ … ≺ E_m ∋ g. Claim A: no segment is entered before its gate was opened from upstream, and each exit segment E_j is first entered from some σ ∈ C_j not closed at that time, through its door. Claim B: before the hero first reaches K₀, he has only walked the P graph; define α(x_i) by the branch he passed; every σ of a false literal is closed by then. Hence if g is reached, α satisfies every clause. K→P moves only close more paths. ∎ The NO side uses no hunger at all, so it also holds under R3.

RNG side effects (§6, :617-637) [conj, source-backed]: prayer at Luck 0 fixes only major troubles; angry gods may attach a ball and chain, which cannot plug holes and gives a throw range of 1. Kicking boulders does nothing; cookies only print a random text.

Dlvl 6

Novelty and prior work

No paper, thesis, wiki page or forum post found proves any complexity result about NetHack; the hardness cores are known theorems, so the new part is the faithful embedding into real NetHack rules and the hunger horizon.

Explained
  • PSPACE result: novel as far as found. The MIT Hardness Group's Push-1 paper (ICGA Journal, 2026) names "roguelike games such as NetHack" as having block puzzles but proves nothing about NetHack. Reviewers may call the result a corollary of Push-1F; the contribution is showing NetHack's real rules (squeezing, diagonals, Sokoban flag, prayer, monsters) match it, plus membership.
  • NP result: novel for NetHack, known as a technique. "A polynomial time limit moves a game into NP while hardness survives" is standard: Lemmings is NP-complete with a time limit and PSPACE-complete without. The new part is proving that faithful hunger really is a polynomial limit, which needed rule S.
  • Any roguelike: the only prior complexity claim found is Thompson's Spelunky paper (FDG 2015, NP-hard via metatheorems).
