Is NetHack EXPTIME-hard when the dice play against you?

Ivan Miziuk · California State University, Chico · companion to the PSPACE/NP paper

Work in progress. This page follows a proof attempt that is still running. The headline statement (“this NetHack language is EXPTIME-complete”) is a conjecture: one building block, a reusable boulder gadget that cannot be broken, does not exist yet. Everything below is labelled with how sure we are.

For everyone · about 8 minutes · no math needed

What if the dungeon fights back?

Our first paper asked how hard it is to win a NetHack level if luck is always on your side. This page asks the opposite: what if every roll of the dice goes against you?

Honest label: this is unfinished research by a student. One part has been tested in the real game, some parts are careful arguments, and the main claim is still a guess we are trying to prove.

1. Two kinds of “hard”

Computer scientists sort problems by how much work a computer needs as the problem gets bigger. Some problems are quick (“polynomial time”). Some need exponential time: add a few more pieces and the work doubles, again and again. Problems of that second kind are called EXPTIME problems.

Here is something surprising. You may have heard of the famous open question “P versus NP”: nobody knows whether some puzzles really need a slow search. For EXPTIME there is no such doubt. Mathematicians proved decades ago that the hardest EXPTIME problems can never be solved quickly, by any program, ever. So if we show NetHack is “EXPTIME-hard”, we know for certain there is no fast general way to solve it.

2. Why you need an opponent

A puzzle you play alone, like a big Sokoban level, is hard but has a short kind of answer: a list of moves. Games against an opponent are different. In chess or Go on a huge board, your plan must answer every reply the other side could make, and that tree of “if they do this, I do that” can be exponentially large. The classic EXPTIME-hard problems are exactly such games: generalized chess, checkers and Go.

NetHack has only one player. But it has dice. If we imagine the dice as an opponent who always picks the worst roll for you (and sees everything you see), we get a two-player game: you against the dungeon. Researchers call this reading “R3”. The question becomes: can you win no matter what the dice do?

3. The dungeon's move: a teleport coin

To give the dungeon a move, we use a real NetHack trap: the teleportation trap. Normally it throws you anywhere on the level. A built-in NetHack rule (used in the Wizard's Tower) can fence teleports into a small zone. Put a trap and exactly two free cells, A and B, inside such a zone, and every time you step on the trap you land on A or on B. That is a coin flip, and in our game the coin is the enemy.

One snag: a careful hero sometimes “escapes” a trap they know about, and then nothing happens. A dungeon that can say “nothing happens” forever could stop you forever. So the hero wears cursed fumble boots, which make that escape impossible. We tested this in the real game 1,200 times: always A or B, never anything else.

4. The plan, and where it is stuck

The plan is to build, out of boulders and corridors, a NetHack level that plays a known hard opponent game (a formula game, Play 2 below). Most pieces are ready: the coin works, the bookkeeping proofs are written, a computer checker exists. The missing piece is a boulder gadget that works like a one-way door and can be used again and again without ever jamming. We have one that works if you check at the end that it never jammed; we have not found one that simply cannot jam, even after trying about 1.4 million layouts by computer. Maybe you can: try the Gadget lab at the bottom.

1The claim and the game

Conjecture: the hunger-off NetHack language LOFFW+C (boulders, Sokoban rules, plus one teleport-trap “coin” and cursed fumble boots) is EXPTIME-complete when every die roll is chosen by an adversary (R3). Membership is proved; hardness is proved for an abstract gadget game and transfers to NetHack once a reusable game-safe self-closing door (or diode + crossover) exists.

The paper shows that with hunger off, deciding whether the hero can reach a cell is PSPACE-complete. There the dice are on the player's side (R2): the player may pick every roll. Here the dice are an enemy who knows everything (R3). A one-player puzzle becomes a two-player game, and two-player games with unbounded length can be EXPTIME-complete. Since P ≠ EXPTIME is a theorem, EXPTIME-hardness would be an unconditional lower bound.

Why the old language cannot be EXPTIME-hard. In LOFFW the reductions satisfy answer-level determinism: on the produced levels no die roll matters, so R2 and R3 give the same answers and the problem stays in PSPACE. To get alternation we must give the dice a real, positional choice. The study surveyed doors (every roll is a veto nature can repeat forever: FAILED), crawling out of water (nature can always return the hero to the entry: FAILED), boulders into pools (each use consumes a boulder, so only polynomially many choices: PSPACE at best), unrestricted teleport (global: FAILED), monsters (large audit, hero can swap with peacefuls), and found the restricted teleport coin VERIFIED in the real game.

The new language. LOFFW+C = LOFFW without the noteleport flag, plus teleport traps inside one des.teleport_region{…, exclude = Z} zone and one cursed pair of fumble boots, read under R3 only. Under R2 the same language stays in PSPACE, so a proof would give a clean separation: same levels, R2 PSPACE-complete, R3 EXPTIME-complete (assuming PSPACE ≠ EXPTIME).

Source problem. Stockmeyer and Chandra's game G6: a CNF formula over your variables X and the opponent's Y; players alternately flip at most one own variable or pass; you win if the formula ever becomes true. It is EXPTIME-complete, its objective is reachability (like ours), and the opponent's move is a menu of 2|Y|+1 options, which a binary coin can pick in ⌈log2(2|Y|+1)⌉ flips. Play 2 lets you play it.

Literature gap. The closest published model, two-player motion planning with one shared agent (Ani, Demaine, Hendrickson, Lynch), is only known to be PSPACE-hard and in EXPTIME; EXPTIME-hardness there is open, and the same gap holds for reachability switching games. So no theorem can be cited for “one agent + nature forks” and the reduction has to be built here.

Why it is a clean coin. This is the closest faithful run possible; it is not a non-wizard run.

Play 2

The formula game (G6)

You own the variables x; the computer owns y. On your turn flip one x (or pass); then the computer flips one y (or passes). You win the moment the formula F becomes true. If it never does, the computer wins. The computer plays perfectly: it has solved every position.

Game

In game A there is a plan that wins whatever the computer does. In game B there is none, even though F could become true if the computer were careless. Telling these apart is the hard question, and for big formulas no fast method can exist.

This is G6 (Stockmeyer and Chandra 1979), EXPTIME-complete. A solver must in general explore the whole position graph, 2|X|+|Y|+1 positions, because play can cycle and last exponentially long. The NetHack reduction turns each move into a walk: your flip is a corridor you choose; the opponent's move is a sequence of ⌈log2(2|Y|+1)⌉ coin flips; the formula check is a corridor of doors you can only pass if every clause has a true literal.

Rule detail: G6 says I wins if F ever becomes true, also right after the opponent's move. Lemma L1 (Section 5) shows it is enough to check F only right after your own moves (you can pass), which is why the NetHack checker is reachable only from the post-move point P.

2How the reduction works

Each G6 round is a loop through the level: your write path, the check point P, then nature's turn as a walk through one coin, and back to the hub. Doors remember the assignment; the checker is a dead end that ends only in the goal.

Doors remember the assignment. Every occurrence of a literal in a clause gets a self-closing door that is open iff the literal is true. Setting x := 1 walks a write path that forces every ¬x door shut (open it, then walk through it, which closes it) and lets you open the x doors. Declining to open a door only hurts you.

Nature picks the opponent's move with one coin. The menu {pass, flip y1, …} is a binary tree of depth d. You cannot carry the tree position in your head (the hero has no memory the dungeon must respect), so each tree node u has a context door Ku: open Ku, flip the coin, then at A (or B) only the branch behind the open Ku is passable, and passing it closes Ku. Exactly one context door is open at a time, so the leaf you reach is a function of the coin flips only.

Why one coin per level. A level has one teleport_region record pair, so at most three bounded zones exist; one coin with context doors simulates any number of nature vertices, so one is all the construction uses.

The checker is a dead end. From P you either go to the checker or to nature. In the checker every clause is a fan of literal doors; with F false some clause has all doors closed and you are stuck forever (a loss). So trying the checker too early never helps.

Rules for the coin tile (from the adversary audit). Z1: no boulder can ever reach the coin zone, its entry E, or their neighbours (a boulder pushed onto a teleport trap is relocated anywhere on the level). Z2: the tile is [A wall T wall B], T's only free neighbour is E, A and B leave by their own corridors; then a “dead” coin is a harmless bounce. Z3: no intended walk over an unfilled pit (pit damage is permanent under R3, which never lets HP regenerate). Z4: never step on T while the turn counter is ≤ 0. Z4 exists because of a finding of the audit: after the 64-bit turn counter wraps, noteleport_level is true (it compares stasis_until = 0 ≥ moves), so for about 263 turns the trap does nothing; the hero simply waits that phase out. VERIFIED source lines, repair STRONG.

Size. O(N4) cells (with the check path and Z4 waits), O(N2) crossovers, one coin, polynomial-time computable (N = formula size + variables). Crossovers sit on write paths that are used exponentially often, so no single-use crossover is allowed anywhere.

“Sound” means the gadget never lets you do something the ideal one-way door forbids. “Complete” means it lets you do everything the ideal door allows. A winner needs both, in every arrangement the boulders can reach.

Rules (NetHack's Sokoban rules with a heavy hero, = Push-1F): the hero moves to any of 8 neighbours; an orthogonal step into a boulder pushes it if the cell beyond is free floor; no diagonal pushes; a diagonal step needs a free target and at least one free corner (walls and boulders both block corners). A traversal starts on a port and ends the first time the hero stands on a port again. The gadget is rejected (LEAK) if any boulder can reach a port cell.

What “game-safe” means here. Inside a gadget only the hero acts (the coin is outside every gadget), so a concrete gadget can replace the ideal one in the dice game iff two one-player simulations hold: sound (every concrete traversal p→q is matched by an ideal traversal p→q with related successors, or is a bounce back to p with no real change) and complete (every ideal traversal is realized), both from the initial states. This is the greatest-fixed-point check of A_core.check, ported line by line; the lab reproduces python tools/exptime/A_check.py exactly on the library (same states, relations, verdicts). Checkable mode: states where the checking sequence is legal are “clean”; the lab verifies that broken is absorbing and compares the clean part, with the joined ports wired by a free corridor, to the target. Caps: 60 floor cells, 5,000 boulder states, 3 million hero positions; beyond that it says “too big”.

5Definitions and proofs

Definition 5.1(the R3 game GI). Fix an instance I run through the harness. A configuration is the full program state at a request for an input token or a core random number RND(x) (the generator state is dropped: nature chooses values directly). Player nodes are input requests, nature nodes are RND(x) calls with successors 0..x−1. Full information. The hero wins a play iff it reaches a win node (top-level command request, hero alive on level 1 at g); terminal non-win nodes (death, leaving the level) and infinite plays are losses. LXR3 = instances whose initial configuration is winning for the hero.

Theorem 5.2(membership, Thm M). If the whitelist X satisfies C1 (poly-size state), C2 (each segment runs poly time with capped RNG loops), C3 (bounded branching), C4 (decidable win/death), C5 (single level), C6 (fixed external inputs), then LXR3 ∈ EXPTIME. The audit checks C1–C6 for W+C. STRONG

Proof

Build the game graph: at most 2p(n) configurations (C1), edges by simulating one segment (C2), out-degree ≤ 231 (C3). Compute the hero's attractor of the win nodes: Attr0 = win nodes; Attrk+1 adds player nodes with some successor in Attrk and nature nodes with all successors in Attrk. The fixpoint comes after at most |V| rounds, each 2O(p(n)) time. The hero wins iff the start is in Attr: reachability games are positionally determined, and nature's strategy “stay outside Attr” beats the hero otherwise, infinite plays being losses. Equivalently, an alternating machine with polynomial space decides it, and APSPACE = EXPTIME (Chandra, Kozen, Stockmeyer 1981). For W+C: the boots, HFumbling timer and trap records are O(1) words, no creator is added, safe_teleds is at most 40 tries plus one scan, and a teleport trap never changes the level. ∎

Definition 5.3(G6, Stockmeyer–Chandra 1979, as restated by Hearn 2006). Instance: a CNF formula F over disjoint X ∪ Y and an assignment α0. Players I and II alternate; a move of I (II) changes at most one variable in X (Y); passing is allowed. Player I wins iff F ever becomes true; an infinite play is not a win for I. G6 is EXPTIME-complete.

Lemma 5.4(L1, post-I checking). Let G6' be G6 except that I wins iff F is true directly after one of I's moves (a pass included). From any start where F(α0) is false, I wins G6 iff I wins G6'. STRONG

Proof

A G6' win is a G6 win. Conversely let σ win G6 and define σ': play σ until the first position where F is true; if it is post-I, done; otherwise it is post-II and σ' passes, giving a post-I position with the same assignment and F true. Every σ'-play follows a σ-play up to that point, so it reaches such a position. ∎

Definition 5.5(one-agent nature game 𝒢). A directed route graph of locations (corridor cells, hero forks, merges, gadget ports, start s, goal g, one coin vertex T with out-ports A, B). Gadgets: the self-closing door SCD with bit σ; tunnel O always passable, afterwards σ' ∈ {σ, open} (hero's choice); tunnel S passable iff open, afterwards closed (forced); MSCD(a,s) one bit with a O-tunnels and s S-tunnels; the coin: arriving at T the play continues at A or B, nature's choice, every time; the crossover: two independent directed passages. Positions = hero location + all bits; full information; the hero wins iff he reaches g; infinite plays lose.

Lemma 5.6(TS, tunnel sharing). MSCD(a, s) is simulated by one SCD D plus, for each tunnel kind with k ≥ 2 users, a sharing region of context SCDs and diodes: every realized hero option is an ideal option or leads to a stuck pocket, and every ideal traversal is realized. STRONG

Proof

User p arrives at up → O-tunnel of context door Kp (closed) → diode → merge m → D's tunnel τ → split m' → for each q: S-tunnel of Kq → diode → vq. Invariant: outside the region all K are closed. Entering at up only Kp's O-tunnel is in reach; between m and m' the only move is through τ (if τ is a closed S-tunnel the hero is stuck behind a diode); at m' only an open K passes, i.e. at most Kp, and passing closes it. So the hero leaves only at vp, having traversed τ once, all K closed again. A declined Kp means stuck at m'. Turning back at a closed S-tunnel becomes “stuck”; with full information the completeness proof never needs it. ∎

Lemma 5.7(N, nature module integrity). If the hero passes Nin with all context doors closed, then he either never leaves the nature module (a loss) or enters exactly the leaf route of the leaf reached from the root by nature's coin outcomes c1…cd, after exactly d coin uses, with all context doors closed. STRONG

Proof

Invariant Jk at the k-th arrival at T: the open context doors are a subset of {Kuk}, uk the depth-(k−1) node on the path c1…ck−1. For k = 1 only Kroot's O-tunnel lies between Nin and the coin entry. Step: nature picks ck, say A; the only continuation is HA, from which only SA-tunnels leave, passable only for open doors, i.e. only Kuk's (if the hero declined to open it he is stuck). Passing it closes it and leads to uk+1; if internal, the only O-tunnel there is Kuk+1's, then back to T: Jk+1. At a leaf (k = d) he is in its leaf route with all K closed. Diodes forbid returning from A/B to T or reaching another demux branch. ∎

Lemma 5.8(literal invariant Inv-L). Let αt be the logical assignment (updated when the hero leaves a write path W(v := b)). Whenever the hero is not inside a write path, the set of open literal doors is contained in Open(αt) = {Dℓ,j : ℓ true under αt}; if he opens every O-tunnel he passes, equality holds. STRONG

Proof

Literal doors change only in write paths (O1, O2, S1) and in the checker (S2, which only closes, and after which the hero never leaves except at g). In W(v := b) only v's doors change: each door of the false literal passes O2 then S1 and is therefore closed at the exit; doors of the true literal may be opened. Leaving a write path elsewhere than its exit is impossible (directed tunnels; a declined O2 makes S1 impassable). ∎

Theorem 5.9(T1, abstract correctness). Player I wins G6(F, α0) iff the hero wins the game 𝒢F built from SCD/MSCD literal doors, write paths, the hub, check point P, the dead-end checker, and the nature module with one coin and ≤ 4|Y|+1 context doors. STRONG

Proof

Completeness. Take I's positional winning strategy σ in G6' (Lemma 5.4). At the hub with assignment α (doors canonical, all K closed) walk the write path of σ(α), opening every O-tunnel; at P the doors equal Open(α'). If F(α') holds, enter the checker: each clause has an occurrence with a true literal, its door is open, pass it; reach g. Otherwise go to Nin, open Kroot, and at each demux take the one open branch (Lemma 5.7); after d flips walk the leaf route of nature's chosen move, back at the hub with Open(α''). Every nature strategy produces a G6' play consistent with σ, which reaches a post-I position with F true, so the hero reaches g. Plays may be exponentially long, which only requires every gadget to be reusable.

Soundness. Let τ be II's positional strategy under which F is never true. Nature, when the hero passes Nin with logical assignment α', fixes a leaf z labelled τ(α') and answers the coin flips along the root-to-z path. By the route structure and Lemma 5.7 every play is a sequence of rounds hub → I-route → P → nature walk → leaf z → hub, so the round-boundary assignments form a τ-play: F is false at every visit of P. If the hero enters the checker there, some clause is false, all its doors are closed (Lemma 5.8), the checker has no O-tunnel, so he is stuck. g has no other in-edge; infinite plays lose. ∎

Definition 5.10(game-safety of a boulder gadget). A gadget is a window with port cells; local state = boulder set; a traversal from port p starts on p and ends the first time the hero stands on a port. A concrete gadget C game-safely realizes an ideal automaton A if (initial states related in both) there are greatest relations with: sound: (c, a) ∈ R ⇒ every concrete traversal p→q (c→c') is matched by an ideal traversal p→q (a→a') with (c', a') ∈ R, or q = p and (c', a) ∈ R; complete: every ideal traversal is realized with related successors. Requirements G1–G5 of the reduction: locality (no boulder reaches a port), soundness, broken absorbing, completeness by RNG-free walks, zero HP and unbounded reuse.

Lemma 5.11(pruning). In a reachability game, let Doom be the positions from which the goal is unreachable in the underlying graph. Deleting hero edges into Doom does not change the winner from any position outside Doom. STRONG

Proof

A winning strategy never enters Doom, so it survives the deletion; deleting hero edges only removes hero options, and nature's edges are untouched, so no losing position becomes winning. Application: if broken gadgets are absorbing and every route to g passes every gadget's check traversals, a broken gadget is in Doom, and only the hero's own pushes can break one; so the game equals the one with all breaking traversals deleted, i.e. the postselected gadget. ∎

Theorem 5.12(T2, NetHack realization, conditional). If the SCD, diode and directed crossover are realized by boulder gadgets meeting G1–G5 (plus a guarded global check path if they are only checkable), and the audit items C-1 to C-4 hold (C-4 in its repaired form with rules Z1–Z4), then the hero has an R3-winning strategy in the NetHack instance IF iff he wins 𝒢F. With Theorem 5.2, LOFFW+C under R3 would be EXPTIME-complete. STRONG conditional; overall CONJECTURE

Proof sketch

(⇐) The ideal strategy is realized by RNG-free walks (G4); the boots are worn first; by C-1 the only roll that matters is the landing cell, which is exactly the abstract coin (witness W9). No HP is ever lost (G5, zero coin damage), so plays of any length survive. (⇒, contrapositive) Nature plays the abstract counter-strategy on the image of clean histories and arbitrary legal values elsewhere. Every realized hero option is an abstract option, a null visit, a stuck pocket, a break (which never wins, Lemma 5.11 with the check path), a death or leaving the level. So a realized play reaching g would project to an abstract counter-strategy play reaching g, contradicting T1. ∎

Lemma 5.14(overshoot). If a boulder is moved p→q = p+d by a push and later back q→p, then right after the first push the hero can push it on to q+d unless a boulder already occupies q+d. STRONG

Proof

The return push is made from q+d, so q+d is floor. After the first push the hero stands on p with the boulder on q; pressing d again pushes it to q+d whenever q+d holds no boulder. A guard boulder on q+d must leave before the return push and come back afterwards, which recreates the problem for the guard. ∎
